What changes when the unit of a school map is the educational program rather than the bold institution heading? In this directory, a seemingly minor modeling decision makes small programs—and many girls—visible.

Scope. Chicago city entries in “Elementary and Parochial High Schools,” printed pages 92–110 of the 1921 Directory of Catholic Colleges and Schools. The selection ends before Argo. Dedicated universities, colleges, high schools, and institutional schools elsewhere in the volume are outside this study.

The page makes some schools easy to miss.

The directory was compiled for the National Catholic Welfare Council and arranges educational institutions geographically and administratively. It is unusually workable for a small extraction project: addresses, school types, and enrollment totals recur in recognizable patterns. Its typography, however, distributes information unevenly. Bold capitals announce an institution; indented paragraphs quietly report another educational program. A quick list of names captures the first level more easily than the second.

The Chicago subsection offers a continuous, bounded selection rather than scattered examples chosen to fit a conclusion. Its 200 institution headings meet the individual assignment’s approximate data target before additional reporting units are included. The choice also supports a question about representation: which educational opportunities become hard to see when a source is flattened into a simple list of schools?

This Catholic administrative directory represents institutional priorities, not pupils’ experiences or all Chicago education. Its publication year does not establish a common census date for every enrollment.

The preface makes the administrative purpose unusually clear. A department established in 1919 approved the directory project in February 1920 and a preliminary report that October. Its focus on the number, variety, and organization of institutions makes administrators legible while pupils’ circumstances remain absent.

A different unit changes the story.

The final table contains 235 reporting units: 199 elementary records, 32 parochial high-school subentries, and four kindergarten records. One kindergarten has its own heading; three are additional subentries. Consequently, 200 headings become 235 rows without inventing 35 buildings. Stable institution identifiers connect related rows while a separate record identifier distinguishes programs.

The distinction matters at St. Alphonsus, where 1,578 elementary pupils and 93 high-school pupils occupy separate reports inside one entry. At St. Columbkille’s, 712 elementary pupils accompany a commercial high-school subentry reporting 24 girls. At St. Stanislaus Kostka’s, the printed totals are 2,728 elementary pupils and just 14 high-school girls. An institution-level pin could conceal the small program even when the location itself appears correctly.

These are counts of separately reported subentries, not a complete inventory of high-school offerings. St. Bernard’s heading, for example, includes the high-school abbreviation without a separate enrollment paragraph. The original program-code field preserves that distinction. A missing subentry should not be converted into a claim that a program did not exist.

Girls dominate these reported subentries.

The 32 high-school subentries report 2,211 pupils. Thirty supply a sex breakdown, accounting for 2,072 pupils: 1,439 girls and 633 boys. Girls therefore represent 69.4% of the known-sex total. Two subentries, totaling 139 pupils, do not provide that breakdown and are excluded from this percentage. Of the 30 classifiable subentries, 25 have more girls than boys, four have more boys, and one is equal.

The programs are often small. Fifteen report 33 pupils or fewer; the median is 40.5. Their size helps explain how they can recede into a directory dominated visually by larger elementary institutions. The contrast invites further research into parish-linked secondary education, especially commercial courses. It does not establish why families enrolled, what pupils could afford, or how schooling affected later employment.

A simple sensitivity check tests the effect of the 139 unclassified pupils. Assigning all of them to boys would leave girls at 65.1% of the 2,211 reported enrollments; assigning all to girls would raise their share to 71.4%. These are arithmetic bounds under the source’s two-category framework, not a statistical confidence interval or a claim about individual identities. They show that the observed female majority does not depend on distributing the unclassified counts in a favorable way. The narrower 69.4% figure still describes only entries with a printed breakdown.

Careful linking is essential. A high-school paragraph reporting 12 boys appears directly after the St. Aloysius girls’ elementary entry. St. Michael’s and St. Patrick’s also place mixed-sex high-school statistics beneath girls’ headings. Assigning pupil sex from the nearest school name would corrupt the result. The table preserves printed sex counts and labels those high-school rows as shared-address subentries, with a reading note.

Likewise, the ratio between elementary and high-school enrollment is not a transition rate. The directory offers a snapshot of different age groups, and this selection excludes separately listed high schools elsewhere in the book. Establishing educational access would require additional evidence about admissions, residence, fees, age, and alternative institutions. The defensible finding concerns the composition of these reported subentries.

An extraction that can be checked.

Codex helped design a source-specific parser, structure the OCR, and compare the selected page images with the resulting table. Only individual selected scans were inspected; the entire PDF was not submitted to a model. Line-break repair and heading detection produced an initial table. Scan review then corrected numeric readings, recovered missed fields, and checked the relation between main entries and subentries.

The correction log records 34 numeric field changes, including structural reconciliation as well as OCR corrections. For example, OCR reads St. Benedict’s elementary total as 675; the scan reads 575, agreeing with 287 boys plus 288 girls. At St. Patrick’s on Commercial Avenue, separately stated academic and commercial high-school subtotals are combined explicitly: 81 plus 15 gives 96. The raw extracted fields remain beside the reviewed values.

Some disagreements belong to the printed book. St. Anne’s reports 857 pupils while its boys and girls add to 877. The Long Avenue St. Stanislaus entry reports 783 while its components add to 683. Both remain flagged rather than silently repaired. A missing enrollment also remains blank. At St. Michael’s kindergarten, the heading says “Kind.” while the entry also prints “Grades, 8”; the explicit kindergarten label is retained with a note.

Stewart and Sinha distinguish usable structured output from field-level accuracy and demonstrate the importance of segmentation and validation. This project applies that distinction: a valid CSV can still contain an incorrect number or relationship. The review here remains Codex-assisted, without an independent human gold standard. It supports an inspectable small study rather than a general accuracy claim about AI.

A coordinate is another interpretation.

The mapping stage locates 163 institution references, representing 194 reporting units at 159 distinct coordinates. Census address-range matching supplies 125 institution references; shared nodes in Chicago’s street-centerline data supply 38 intersection references. Directions and street-type expansions used in those queries are documented assumptions. All locations are modern references, not verified 1921 school parcels.

This distinction matters because historical addresses do not automatically survive into contemporary gazetteers. The Chicago History Museum’s guidance explains the importance of street renaming and renumbering. Ambiguous matches, incomplete street descriptions, and unresolved historic names remain unlocated. St. Gabriel’s is particularly instructive: its high school reports 94 pupils, including 75 girls, but the selected entry provides no street address. Losing that pin must not erase those pupils from the analysis.

Gregory, Donaldson, Hardie, and Rayson connect mapping to repeated movement between textual evidence and spatial abstraction. Their discussion also cautions that a precise point can simplify an ambiguous place reference. Accordingly, the reader can inspect source pages and unresolved records as well as markers. Co-located records are grouped without random displacement; no invented movement lines or pupil catchments are drawn.

The mapped pattern is uneven.

Splitting the 163 mapped institution headings at their median latitude (41.85795°) gives 81 southern and 82 northern headings. Their high-school subentries differ: girls account for 88.6% of reported sex-classified enrollment in the southern half, versus 56.6% in the northern half. The northern half’s 139 unclassified pupils leave its possible girls’ share between 50.7% and 61.1%, still below the southern figure.

This is an exploratory geographic association, not an explanation of neighborhood opportunity. The split defines relative halves of this dataset, not historical districts. Four unmapped high-school subentries, modern location uncertainty, and schools outside the selection limit the comparison. The stronger conclusion is that the subsection’s overall female majority is geographically uneven among mapped reports.

What the model makes possible.

The dataset follows Broman and Woo’s organizational principles: one reporting unit per row, explicit variables, stable identifiers, and a separate data dictionary. Historical transcription, interpretive notes, and modern location evidence occupy different columns. Personal staff names are omitted from the public table; institution names remain necessary for historical identification. No individual pupil records or inferred identities are created.

The book’s own abbreviation key provides another safeguard: printed page 8 expands “Comm. H. S.” as “Commercial High School.” The table uses that historical label without treating “commercial” as evidence of present-day for-profit ownership. Preserving both a normalized program category and the original abbreviation makes the interpretation reversible. Another researcher can regroup the records without losing the textual distinction that first motivated the analysis.

Scaling the work would require independent transcription checks, a fuller treatment of shared school structures, and historically appropriate address research. Comparing several editions could then test whether these small programs expanded, contracted, or changed their reported sex composition. For now, the central result is methodological as well as historical: the apparently tidy decision to extract one school per heading would make a substantial part of this subsection’s educational geography difficult to see.

Extraction brief, iteration log, and prompts

The initiating request asked Codex to choose a manageable source and find an overlooked pattern. The following reusable parsing brief states the rules implemented during this session. This was a Codex-assisted coding and scan-review workflow, not a benchmark in which the same prompt was sent to several model APIs.

Work only on the Chicago city subsection, printed pages 92–110.
Extract one main reporting unit per institution heading and separate rows
for explicitly reported high-school and kindergarten subentries.
Keep a stable institution ID and a distinct record ID. Preserve source page,
historical address, raw values, reviewed values, and reading notes.
Use blank for unreported enrollment. Never infer pupil sex from a school name.
Keep printed arithmetic disagreements. Do not invent coordinates.
Label modern address/intersection matches separately from historical evidence.
Exclude personal staff names from public data. Return a rectangular table.

Actual refinements: initial heading segmentation was expanded to capture damaged brackets and mixed-case OCR; student-field extraction was checked against the individual scans; ambiguous shared-address subentries received explicit notes; modern street intersections supplemented address-range matching. The linked scripts and correction log expose these decisions.
