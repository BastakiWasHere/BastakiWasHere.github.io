## Why I chose Iceland

I chose Iceland because it has many distinctive features for a relatively small country. The contrast between its name and its volcanic landscape immediately interested me. A country called “Iceland” having volcanoes and hot springs feels like a geographical plot twist. Its glaciers and waterfalls added to that interest. My existing impression that many people live near the coast raised another question: how might the landscape relate to where people live and travel?

Those interests shaped the five categories selected for this map: waterfalls, glaciers, hot springs, volcanoes, and casinos. The first four reflect the landscape that attracted me. Casinos introduce a human activity into the comparison. A casino is an unexpected guest at a meeting of glaciers and volcanoes, but that contrast also tests what a geographic database can tell us about different kinds of places.

The map offers a starting point for these questions. Without population distributions, visitor numbers, and transport networks, it cannot establish how the selected features influence settlement or tourism.

## From country file to selected landscape

The Iceland GeoNames file downloaded on 24 September 2026 contains **16,296 records**. Selecting five feature codes produces **190 records: 114 waterfalls, 46 glaciers, 15 hot springs, 14 volcanoes, and one casino**. These are records in this snapshot, not a complete inventory of Iceland.

The full dataset complicates the dramatic image that initially attracted me. Its most frequent code is `FRM`, representing farms, with 4,656 records. Hills, streams, and mountains are also numerous. My selection emphasizes striking natural features while excluding a substantial geography of everyday land use. Choosing farms instead of volcanoes would tell a different story about the same country.

The notebook imports the 19-column file, assigns column types, filters feature codes, and exports a Leaflet map. Popups expose names, coordinates, recorded elevations, modification dates, and links to individual records. Waterfalls comprise 60% of the selected points, so hiding them helps reveal smaller categories. Interaction changes what becomes noticeable.

## Patterns worth looking at

Glacier records occur in several groups. Southeastern examples include Breiðamerkurjökull, Skaftafellsjökull, and Skeiðarárjökull; northern examples include Tungnahryggsjökull and Unadalsjökull. This distribution complicates a simple mental picture of Iceland’s ice occupying one continuous location. However, a named point cannot represent a glacier’s area or changing boundaries.

Waterfalls appear across multiple regions. Skógafoss and Seljalandsfoss provide southern examples, while Dettifoss and Goðafoss occur farther north. Their distribution contrasts with the grouped glacier records. Nearby points invite questions about water and ice, but proximity cannot establish that a particular glacier feeds a particular waterfall. That would require drainage or catchment data.

The selected layers make parts of southern and southwestern Iceland especially prominent, while the glacier layer also draws attention northward. This describes visibility in my selected map. It does not establish which regions have the best GeoNames coverage: a sparse display could reflect the landscape, my filters, or incomplete recording. Coverage quality would require comparison with an independent inventory.

## Unusual discoveries: missing ice and repeated names

One revealing discovery is that **Vatnajökull exists in the country file but is absent from the selected glacier layer**. Its code is `CAPG`, meaning icecap, while the filter selects `GLCR`, meaning glacier. Geysers likewise have a separate code, `GYSR`, distinct from hot springs. These distinctions follow the [GeoNames classification system](https://www.geonames.org/export/codes.html).

A reader might assume that “Glaciers” includes every major ice feature. The filter does something narrower. Vatnajökull’s absence results from classification rather than a missing record. The landscape has not changed; the selection rule has.

Another surprise is that four selected records are named exactly **Gullfoss**, at different coordinates. A familiar name alone cannot identify a particular location. Coordinates and unique GeoNames identifiers matter; matching names should not automatically be treated as accidental duplicates.

The casino layer contains only Háspenna in Reykjavík, last modified on 25 February 2018. That establishes what this download contains, but neither its current operation nor the total number of comparable businesses. A lone green point is a record, not a national gambling census.

A lighter example connects geothermal landscapes to everyday life: at Laugarvatn Fontana, rye bread is baked using heat from the ground. Iceland’s landscape can apparently help prepare lunch as well as provide scenery. This external example makes my interest in human use of the landscape more concrete; it is not a finding derived from the mapped spring points. [Visit Iceland](https://www.visiticeland.com/article/green-circle-iceland/)

## What *Do Maps Lie?* adds

In Sheffield Methods Institute’s *Do Maps Lie?*, two maps use the same London house-price data but produce different impressions. Between **0:28 and 1:22**, an equal-interval classification places most areas in the lowest category, while comparison with the UK average emphasizes London’s high prices. Classification changes the argument without changing the underlying numbers. [Watch the example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G0_MBrJnRq0&t=28s).

My map uses feature categories rather than numerical price intervals, but the example raises a related question: how do presentation choices shape interpretation? Selecting volcanoes, glaciers, and waterfalls emphasizes the dramatic Iceland that first attracted me. Selecting farms and settlements would support a different account.

Symbol choices also matter. Volcano and casino markers are larger to make smaller categories easier to find. Their sizes do not measure physical extent, popularity, or importance. Red volcano markers identify a category rather than an official danger level. Explaining these choices helps readers distinguish recorded information from visual emphasis.

## GeoNames as a data assemblage

Kitchin and Lauriault describe a **data assemblage** as the interconnected social and technical arrangements that produce, manage, analyze, and translate data (2018, p. 8). Table 1-1 includes institutions, infrastructure, practices, standards, and licensing among its components (p. 10).

Applied here, the idea draws attention beyond the finished dots. Source organizations and contributors supply information; GeoNames organizes records; R selects codes; Leaflet supplies symbols and controls; and GitHub Pages presents the result. My interests also shape the selection. Each stage contributes to what readers encounter.

The icecap/glacier distinction makes this chain visible. Kitchin and Lauriault explain that apparently fixed datasets remain open to correction, regrouping, and different interpretations (p. 9). Changing my filter would change the picture of Iceland. This is why a technically functioning map still requires explanation of how its data and categories were assembled.

## Provenance, dates, and missing information

GeoNames’ source directory lists the **National Land Survey of Iceland** and **Registers Iceland** among its Icelandic sources. This provides evidence of institutional contributions, although the country file does not identify the source of every individual row. [GeoNames data sources](https://www.geonames.org/datasources/)

GeoNames also supports user corrections and additions. Its provenance therefore involves both source datasets and subsequent editing. The team page consulted for this draft did not list an Iceland ambassador. That absence does not demonstrate an absence of contributors or reliable source material. [About GeoNames](https://www.geonames.org/about.html); [GeoNames team](https://www.geonames.org/team.html).

Modification dates in the downloaded snapshot are uneven: 6,891 records have a last-modified year of 2016, followed by 2,109 in 2011 and 2,087 in 2020. These describe the latest recorded modifications, not a complete update history or proof of when places were surveyed. They do not, by themselves, explain what caused those concentrations.

Missing information also limits interpretation. **175 of the 190 selected records lack a value in the elevation field.** Missing elevation does not mean zero elevation. These values cannot support a complete elevation comparison. Similarly, a recent download does not make every record recently verified, as the casino’s older modification date illustrates.

## Where this workflow could lead

A future project could compare environmental features with settlements, roads, or tourism infrastructure. That would extend my original question about where people live and travel, but would require compatible datasets and attention to their dates and definitions. Accessibility could then be examined rather than assumed from visual proximity.

A humanities project could instead explore place names and alternative names. The repeated Gullfoss records show why language needs geographic context. A reproducible filtering workflow would make it possible to compare different selections while documenting how each changes the resulting story.

My interest in Iceland began with contrasts between ice, heat, water, and human activity. This project adds another: the complexity of a landscape compared with the simplicity of a dot. Its most useful outcome is a more careful way of asking what those dots represent, how they were selected, and what remains outside the frame.
